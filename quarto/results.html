Reading the maps

Author

Vagish Hemmige

Provisional

Text highlighted like this is provisional: it depends on a decision the analysis plan has not yet made.

This page explains what is on every map and how to read it. Each study then has its own page, with its maps, its regional table and what they show:

  • HCV donors, 2015–2020: one map, each region’s share of the country’s multiorgan heart transplants from hepatitis C virus (HCV) donors (Madan et al. 2021).
  • DCD donors, 2020–2023: two maps, each region’s share of the country’s multiorgan heart transplants from donors after circulatory death (DCD), and its share of all multiorgan heart transplants (Madan et al. 2024).
  • Machine perfusion, 2020–2024: one map, how often each region used ex vivo machine perfusion in heart transplants from donors after brain death (DBD) (Madan et al. 2025).

The maps are drawn by R/main.R from the regional counts published in each paper. The code that draws them is under Code in the sidebar, one page per study.

What is on every map

Every map is built from the same layers, in the same order, so once you can read one you can read them all.

On the map What it shows Where it comes from
Shaded states The value for the state’s UNOS region. Every state in a region has the same colour. The region’s count in the published study
Thin grey lines State borders Census state boundaries, through {sRtr} (Hemmige and Czeresnia 2026)
Thick black lines The borders of the eleven UNOS regions {sRtr}’s region outlines
Navy numbers on pale boxes Each region’s number, 1 to 11 Placed inside each region by the code
Dots Adult heart-transplant centers, at their true locations. They are partly transparent, so where several overlap the dot looks darker. HRSA’s current transplant-center list (Health Resources and Services Administration, n.d.)
Grey states A region with no value Shown as no data, never as zero
Note under the map Where the shading and the dots come from, and the date the center list was downloaded Printed on every map

The {tmap} version adds two things: a red box on the main map around the stretch from Washington, DC, to Boston, and an enlarged copy of that box beside the map. Between those cities, centers sit so close together that their dots overlap on a national map; the enlarged copy separates them.

Light colours are low values and dark red ones are high. The legend gives the numbers. No map on this site uses a grey state: every region has a value in all three studies, even when that value is zero.

Which states are in each region

The Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN), operated by the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS), groups the states into eleven regions. The maps use the grouping in {sRtr}’s region table, which places each state in one region. Alaska, Hawaii and Puerto Rico are not on the maps.

Region States on the maps
1 Connecticut, Maine, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Rhode Island
2 Delaware, District of Columbia, Maryland, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, West Virginia, and Northern Virginia
3 Alabama, Arkansas, Florida, Georgia, Louisiana, Mississippi
4 Oklahoma, Texas
5 Arizona, California, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah
6 Idaho, Montana, Oregon, Washington
7 Illinois, Minnesota, North Dakota, South Dakota, Wisconsin
8 Colorado, Iowa, Kansas, Missouri, Nebraska, Wyoming
9 New York, Vermont
10 Indiana, Michigan, Ohio
11 Kentucky, North Carolina, South Carolina, Tennessee, Virginia (except Northern Virginia)

The regions differ a great deal in size and in how many transplants they do. Region 6 covers four large states in the Northwest but does few transplants; Region 9 is mostly New York State; Region 5 includes California and does the most multiorgan heart transplants of any region. A region’s colour says nothing about its area, but a large region catches the eye, so read the legend and the table rather than the amount of colour.

The Clinical background page says more about the regions, and Methodological background explains how a region that crosses a state line is handled.

Two kinds of percentage

The maps use two different percentages, and they answer different questions. The legend of every map says which one it shows.

Share of the national count, or the region's own rate

A region’s share of the national count is the region’s transplants of one kind divided by all such transplants in the United States. The eleven shares add up to 100%. The HCV and DCD maps show this. It answers the question where in the country were these transplants done?

A region’s own rate is the region’s transplants of one kind divided by the region’s own transplants. The rates do not add up to anything. The machine-perfusion map shows this. It answers the question how often did this region use the technique?

The two can tell different stories about the same region. A worked example from the machine-perfusion study: Region 11 did 129 of the country’s 561 machine-perfusion transplants, the largest share of any region (23%). But it also did many transplants overall, so only 129 of its 1,687 DBD heart transplants (8%) used machine perfusion. Region 1 did fewer machine-perfusion transplants (79, a 14% share), but they were 79 of only 577 transplants, so its own rate (14%) was the highest in the country (Madan et al. 2025).

The general rule: a region that does many transplants of every kind tends to have a large share of any one kind. A high share of the national count is therefore partly a sign of a busy region. To see whether a region used a donor type more than its size would predict, compare its share of that donor type with its share of all transplants. The HCV and DCD tables give both, and their pages make that comparison.

Decision pending: whether to add maps of each region’s own rate for the HCV and DCD studies, beside the published share maps (D3 in SETUP-NOTES.md).

Each map has its own colour scale

The colour scale of each map runs from that map’s lowest value to its highest. Dark red on the HCV map means about 28%; on the machine-perfusion map it means about 14%. Colours cannot be compared from one map to another, only within a map. To compare across maps, read the legends or the tables.

What the maps cannot tell you

  • Nothing about a single state. The whole region carries one value. A dark state may have done none of the region’s transplants; a neighbouring state in the same region may have done all of them.
  • Not why regions differ. The studies described variation; they did not test what caused it. Differences may reflect a few centers’ practice, local donor supply, distances, or chance where numbers are small.
  • Not which centers took part. The dots are adult heart-transplant centers in HRSA’s list on the day the maps were drawn, not the centers that existed during each study period, and not the centers that did the transplants mapped. A center that opened after a study ended still has a dot; one that closed has none. This was decided for the teaching purpose of the maps (D1 in SETUP-NOTES.md; plan §4).
  • Not how many transplants a center did. Every dot is the same size. A region with many dots does not necessarily do many transplants.
  • Not how certain the values are. Where a region did only a few transplants of a kind, one or two more or fewer would change its colour a lot. The tables give the counts so this can be judged.

The paper behind each map gives the full analysis, including trends over time and patient outcomes, which a regional map cannot show.

Two versions of each map

Each map is drawn twice, and the study pages show both in tabs:

  • With corridor inset ({tmap}), shown first, with the Washington-Boston corridor enlarged beside the main map.
  • Without inset ({ggplot2}), closest in look to the maps in the published papers.

The two show the same values with the same colours. Every map can be downloaded as a PDF, a vector file that stays sharp at any size, from the link under it. TIFF files at 600 dots per inch, for journal submission, are kept with each run of the publishing workflow on GitHub (Actions, the run, then Artifacts).
